# *Helicobacter pylori* Phylogeography : The Paradigmatic Precedent

## Overview

*Helicobacter pylori* is the **canonical example** of a bacterial
pathogen whose population structure faithfully reflects the history
of human migrations. The paradigm was established in two foundational
papers:

- **Falush D., Wirth T., Linz B., Pritchard J.K., Stephens M., Kidd M.,
  Blaser M.J., Graham D.Y., Vacher S., Perez-Perez G.I., Yamaoka Y.,
  Mégraud F., Otto K., Reichard U., Katzowitsch E., Wang X., Achtman M.,
  Suerbaum S. *Traces of human migrations in Helicobacter pylori
  populations.* **Science** 299(5612): 1582–1585 (2003). DOI:
  `10.1126/science.1080857`
- **Linz B., Balloux F., Moodley Y., Manica A., Liu H., Roumagnac P.,
  Falush D., Stamer C., Prugnolle F., van der Merwe S.W., Yamaoka Y.,
  Graham D.Y., Perez-Trallero E., Wadström T., Suerbaum S., Achtman M.
  *An African origin for the intimate association between humans and
  Helicobacter pylori.* **Nature** 445(7130): 915–918 (2007). DOI:
  `10.1038/nature05562`

The Linz 2007 paper showed that **genetic diversity in *H. pylori*
decreases with geographic distance from East Africa**, mirroring the
human out-of-Africa diversity gradient, and supporting the conclusion
that humans were already infected with *H. pylori* when they left
Africa ~58,000 years ago.

The paradigm has since been extended by:
- **Moodley Y. et al. 2009 *Science***, "The peopling of the Pacific
  from a bacterial perspective" (Austronesian expansion traced through
  *H. pylori*). DOI: `10.1126/science.1166083`
- **Moodley Y. et al. 2012 *PLOS Pathog***, "Age of the association
  between *Helicobacter pylori* and man"
- **Maixner F. et al. 2016 *Science***, *The 5300-year-old Helicobacter
  pylori genome of the Iceman.* **Science** 351(6269): 162–165 (2016).
  DOI: `10.1126/science.aad2545`, ancient *H. pylori* from Ötzi.

your MTBC phylogeography work for an MNHN / Éco-anthropologie jury.

   structure inference + genetic diversity gradient → migration

## The seven *H. pylori* populations

The canonical classification (Linz 2007 + subsequent refinements):

| Population | Subpopulations | Geographic correlate | Key references |
|---|---|---|---|
| **hpAfrica1** | hspAfrica1-WA, hspAfrica1-SA | West and South Africa : Bantu expansion carriers | Falush 2003, Linz 2007 |
| **hpAfrica2** |, | San peoples (Khoisan), South Africa, pre-Bantu ancestral African | Linz 2007 |
| **hpNEAfrica** |, | Northeast Africa (Somalia, Sudan, Ethiopia) | Linz 2007 |
| **hpEurope** |, | Europe, Middle East, India, Iran, **recombinant hybrid of AE1 (Central Asian) + AE2 (Northeast African)** | Falush 2003, Moodley 2012 |
| **hpAsia2** |, | North India, Bangladesh, Thailand, Malaysia | Linz 2007 |
| **hpEastAsia** | hspEAsia, hspAmerind, hspMaori | East Asia → Americas (via Beringia) → Pacific (Polynesian) | Falush 2003, Moodley 2009 |
| **hpSahul** |, | Australian and New Guinean Aborigines (isolated population) | Moodley 2009 |

### The four ancestral components (AE)

Moodley 2012 refined the model by identifying **four ancestral
components** whose admixture patterns produce the seven populations:

| Component | Putative origin |
|---|---|
| AE1 | Central Asia |
| AE2 | Northeast Africa |
| AE3 | West Africa |
| AE4 | East Asia |

Modern hpEurope is modelled as an admixture of AE1 + AE2, a result
that parallels the human admixture signal observed in AADR-style
analyses of ancient European populations.

### Out-of-Africa chronology

Linz 2007 estimated the separation time between hpAfrica2 and other
populations at **~58,000 years** (95% CI ~28,000–89,000 years), in
rough agreement with human out-of-Africa dates (~60,000 BP).

## Ancient *H. pylori* genome : the Iceman (Maixner 2016)

- **Ötzi the Iceman**, 5,300 years BP, Alps
- Whole *H. pylori* genome reconstructed from his stomach contents
- Unexpectedly, the Iceman's *H. pylori* was **hpAsia2-like**, not
  hpEurope as modern Europeans
- **Quantitative finding** (Maixner 2016): the Iceman strain contains
  **only 6.5% of the AE2 (northeast African) ancestral component** of
  hpEurope, i.e. it is essentially pure AE1 (Central Asian), with
  the AE2 admixture that defines modern hpEurope having occurred
  *after* his death
- Interpretation: the hybrid hpEurope population arose **after 5 300
  BP**, possibly via Neolithic admixture between AE1 + AE2
- This is the **first and still one of the few** published ancient
  *H. pylori* genomes, analogous to the very small (n=16) ancient
  MTBC corpus today

This single ancient data point is analogous to the small corpus of
ancient MTBC genomes you already have from `spaam-ancient-metagenome-dir`
, it illustrates how a single well-preserved ancient sample can
refine the chronology of a pathogen's population history.

## Methodological template : Linz approach in 6 steps

The approach transposed from *H. pylori* (Linz 2007) to any
host-pathogen system:

1. **Sample geographically.** Obtain pathogen isolates from every
   modern human population, stratified by country / ethnicity /
   language family (use `glottolog` for linguistic stratification).
2. **Sequence MLST or whole-genome.** Classical Linz work used
   7-locus MLST; modern work uses core-genome SNPs.
3. **Infer population structure** with Bayesian clustering
   (STRUCTURE, fineSTRUCTURE, or ADMIXTURE for bacteria).
4. **Test geographic diversity gradient.** Compute per-population
   heterozygosity and regress against geographic distance from a
   putative origin.
5. **Date divergences with a molecular clock** (use `iqtree-lsd2` or
   `beast2-phylogeography`).
6. **Overlay with human host population genetics** (from `aadr`,
   `amtdb`, or published modern data) to test for congruence.

This is **exactly the workflow you are running on MTBC**. The novelty
is in scale (300k vs 1k genomes), in the addition of **agentic AI**
over 150k articles for metadata enrichment, and in the systematic
use of the coevolution constellation.

3. Close on the methodological advance: *"Here we extend the
   pathogen-as-migration-proxy paradigm from MLST-based analyses of
   ~10³ *H. pylori* to whole-genome analyses of ~10⁵ MTBC genomes,
   using agentic AI to integrate heterogeneous metadata sources."*

### Workflow 2 : Comparative slide: *H. pylori* vs *M. tuberculosis*

For a seminar slide that positions your work:

| Aspect | *H. pylori* (Linz 2007) | *M. tuberculosis* (your work) |
|---|---|---|
| Genome type | Highly recombinant | Clonal (effectively no recombination) |
| Sample size | ~370 isolates (Linz) | ~300 000 (TBannotator) |
| Time depth | ~58 000 years (out of Africa) | ~5 000–70 000 years (debated) |
| Population structure | 6 populations + 1 isolated (hpSahul) | 10 lineages (Senelle scheme) |
| Historical signal | Out of Africa, Bantu, Neolithic Europe, Pacific colonisation | Neolithic + colonial + modern |
| Methodological substrate | 7-locus MLST | Whole-genome SNPs |
| AI/text-mining integration | None | TBannotator on 150 000 articles |

### Workflow 3 : Chain with enterobase for per-strain queries

Goal: fetch *H. pylori* strain metadata for direct comparison.

1. Go to `enterobase.warwick.ac.uk` → *Helicobacter* database (see
   the `enterobase` skill).
2. Filter by country or year.
3. Extract HierCC cluster memberships.
4. Compare the HierCC structure with the classical Linz populations.
5. Note that HierCC and Linz populations operate at different
   resolutions: HierCC is a continuous cgMLST-distance clustering,
   while Linz populations are STRUCTURE-based admixture clusters.
   They are not directly interchangeable.

### Workflow 4 : Programmatic fetch via PubMLST REST API

The PubMLST REST API gives free, no-key access to the *H. pylori*
typing database. Verified endpoints (session-fetched):

```bash
# 1. List schemes (the MLST scheme is scheme 1)
curl -sL "https://rest.pubmlst.org/db/pubmlst_helicobacter_seqdef/schemes/1" | python -m json.tool

# 2. Download all profiles (the 4 511 STs)
curl -sL "https://rest.pubmlst.org/db/pubmlst_helicobacter_seqdef/schemes/1/profiles_csv" \
  -o hpylori_profiles.tsv
```

```python
import pandas as pd
prof = pd.read_csv("hpylori_profiles.tsv", sep="\t")
# Columns: ST, atpA, efp, mutY, ppa, trpC, ureI, yphC
print(len(prof), "sequence types")  # 4511 (March 2026 snapshot)
```

The classical MLST profiles can be combined with the population-level
metadata (Falush 2003 / Linz 2007 / Moodley 2009) to build a
population-stratified phylogeny.

### Workflow 5 : Cross-reference with AADR

Goal: for a specific human population from AADR, find the
corresponding *H. pylori* population and discuss the match.

1. Identify a human population in AADR (e.g. Bell Beaker from
   Central Europe).
2. Look up the expected *H. pylori* population for the ethnographic
   descendants (hpEurope).
3. Discuss whether the ancient *H. pylori* (if any exists, so far
   just Ötzi) matches the expected modern population.
4. Transpose the question to MTBC: does the ancient MTBC of the
   same region match modern lineage distributions?

## Caveats

- **H. pylori is recombinant.** Unlike MTBC (clonal), *H. pylori*
  undergoes frequent horizontal gene exchange, so the population
  structure is "fuzzier" : STRUCTURE-based clustering is appropriate
  for *H. pylori* but would be misapplied to MTBC. Do not blindly
  copy the methods.
- **Linz 2007 used MLST, not WGS.** The original paradigm is based on
  7 loci. Modern WGS-based work refines but does not replace the
  original conclusions.
- **Ancient *H. pylori* is still very sparse.** The Iceman is the
  headline case, but few other ancient genomes have been published.
  The ancient MTBC corpus (16 samples in SPAAM) is actually
  **larger** than the ancient *H. pylori* corpus.
- **hpAfrica2 is rare outside southern Africa.** Extending sampling
  to under-represented populations remains an active research area.
- **EnteroBase coverage of *Helicobacter* is smaller** than for
  *Salmonella* or *Yersinia*. For historic sequences, original
  supplementary data from the Linz / Moodley papers are often the
  most reliable source.
